1 Purpose

This guide explains how to prepare, launch, teach, grade, communicate in, and maintain DATA-R 155 as a 7-week online Canvas course. It is written for a future instructor inheriting the course,

Use this guide with the live Canvas course. Canvas is the source of truth for content access, submissions, grades, announcements, dates, communication, and student-facing instructions. Do not rely on external planning websites or draft artifacts as student-facing course infrastructure.

Courses generally open to students the Friday before the first official day of class. This course moves quickly. Set aside time before launch to verify dates, module access, gradebook settings, rubrics, outcomes, discussion groups, and the custom HTML course calendar.

2 Course Operating Principles

DATA-R 155 is a 7-week online Introduction to R course built around repeated Core practice, optional Advanced choices, timely feedback, and specifications grading. The course is not low-touch. It depends on active instructor monitoring, fast Core grading, and proactive student outreach.

2.1 Stable assumptions for the master course

  • Canvas is the source of truth for course content, submissions, grading, announcements, dates, and student communication.

  • The course should be copied forward from the most recent teaching site so successful live edits carry into the next offering.

  • This guide currently describes the 7-week online version. In-person adaptations may be added later, but they are not part of this guide yet.

  • This course usually runs multiple sections at the same time and it is cross-listed for ANTH 155, PSYC 155, SOC 155, BIOL 155, and ANTH 580. All enrollments are managed in one Canvas teaching site when institutionally allowed.

  • Graduate students complete the same grading bundles as undergraduate students. Differentiation comes through Advanced activity options that graduate level students tend to want (e.g., Chapter 9 GitHub).

  • Required student tools are Canvas, R, and RStudio Desktop. Other tools appear inside specific activities and should be reviewed where they affect setup or support. For example, quarto may not work on Newer Windows processors (ARMs), so the instructor should be prepared to support students who encounter that issue in a tool-specific activity.

2.2 Course design in one paragraph

Students complete required Core work to build the foundation for R programming, reproducible workflow, and learning-skill development. Students who want a B or A add Advanced activities from a menu. Advanced activities allow students to choose work aligned with their goals, such as thesis research skill preparation verses the course being required for the degree. The instructor’s main role is to reinforce the weekly Core learning skill in custom and timely feedback and intervenes early when students fall behind.

3.5 Standard release and lock pattern

Item Standard pattern
Course access Canvas opens to students at 12:00 a.m. Mountain Time on the Friday before the official Monday start.
Module access Each module opens at 12:00 a.m. Mountain Time on the Friday before its module week.
Advanced access Advanced items also open on Friday but are only accessible to students who have earned Complete on the required prior module’s Core work.
Core due dates Generally Tuesday/Thursday; Module 1 exception.
Core lock date Saturday night 11:59 p.m. for that week’s Core module.
Advanced lock date Sunday night 11:59 pm. for the prior week’s module.
Time zone Always Mountain Time.

Important timing rule. Due dates and lock dates are not the same. The period between the due date and the lock date is primarily the student revision window after instructor feedback. Students may submit late during that window, but late first submissions submitted after the instructor grading pass may not receive feedback before the lock date, requiring a token for Core revision access. The instructor should grade on the stated rhythm and does not need to chase late submissions before the lock.

4 Weekly Instructor Workflow

This course depends on a timely instructor rhythm. Core completion affects revision windows and Advanced access, so grading is part of the course structure. During active teaching, estimate 12-15 hours per week depending on enrollment, student support needs, and depth of feedback. The scheduled rhythm below is the minimum maintenance rhythm, not a guarantee of total workload.

4.1 Weekly grading and support rhythm

Day Required instructor work Why it matters
Wednesday Quick-grade submitted Core work, including early submissions when available. In Week 1, also contact students who have not logged in or submitted early work. Students need fast feedback while there is still time to revise before the Core lock.
Friday Grade submitted Core work and any submitted Advanced work. Conduct a weekly grade audit. Email/check in with students who are missing current Core work, appear stuck, or have not responded. This is the key intervention point before the Saturday Core lock.
Monday Grade weekend/locked Advanced work. Review late/Not Ready Core items. Apply tokens when needed. Adjust student-specific dates. Conduct another grade audit. Submit academic alerts when appropriate. Advanced work has no token pathway. Monday is also the recovery-planning and escalation day.
Other days Monitor Canvas messages, assignment comments, and meeting bookings. Grade early submissions when it fits your workflow, but do not chase late submissions outside the stated rhythm. The course needs active monitoring, but the instructor also needs a sustainable boundary.

Boundary. If students submit after the Friday grading pass and the Core item locks Saturday, they risk needing a token if the submission is Not Ready. The instructor should grade on the stated rhythm; the instructor does not need to chase every late submission before the lock.

4.2 Weekly grade audit

Every Friday and Monday, after grading, verify that grades, completion statuses, outcomes, and access logic match. In the current setup, this may require checking both the Traditional Gradebook and the Learning Mastery Gradebook/outcomes. The audit prevents students from being blocked from Advanced work or revisions because a status was not updated in the right place.

4.3 Live course edits during teaching

When grading reveals repeated student confusion, make small clarifying edits in Canvas. This is a normal part of teaching the course. For larger changes, document the change in the running course-change memo. After meaningful page or link edits, run link validation.

5 Grading, Specifications Grading, and Feedback

5.1 Specifications grading overview

DATA-R 155 uses Specifications Grading. Students earn final grades by completing bundles of work to a professional standard. There is no partial-credit point chasing. Work either meets the specifications, needs revision, or does not count.

Final grade bundle Requirements
F Missing Core work or fewer than 25 Core items earn Complete.
D At least 25 Core items earn Complete.
C All Core work earns Complete.
B All Core work earns Complete, plus at least 12 Advanced assignments earn Complete.
A All Core work earns Complete, plus at least 18 Advanced assignments earn Complete.
A+ All Core work earns Complete, plus at least 20 Advanced assignments earn Complete, and the student shows exceptional learning growth and engagement.

5.2 Status labels

Work type Status labels Instructor meaning
Core Complete / Not Ready / Missing Core is required. Not Ready does not count until revised; for required Core work, Not Ready functions like Missing until the student fixes it.
Advanced Complete / Not Ready / No Submission Advanced is optional. No Submission means the student did not choose that option. Advanced work has deadlines and no revision tokens after lock date.

5.3 Current Canvas gradebook setup: active design area

Important. At the time this guide was drafted, the course used both the Traditional Gradebook and the Learning Mastery Gradebook. This may change in a future copy of the course. Follow the live Canvas configuration you inherit. The instructor must update grades timely in the Canvas gradebook(s) that control Core completion for Advanced module access.

Component Current use
Traditional Gradebook Graded items are worth 1 point: Complete = 1, Not Ready = 0.5, Missing / No Submission = 0. Note: the threshold for Not Ready to Complete is set at 0.95, but when grading Not Ready, always enter 0.5
Custom grading schemes Core and Advanced use different display language so required missing work and optional non-selected work are not conflated.
Learning Mastery Gradebook / Outcomes Used to track completion/outcomes and support bundle counting/access logic. Core quizzes may auto-grade traditionally but still require Learning Mastering manual entry.
Rubrics Rubrics are attached to assignments and outcomes. If adding an assignment: (1) create an outcome, (2) create matching rubric and attached outcome, and (3) a attach the correct rubric to assignment/quiz/discussion.
Tokens Tracked in a non-grade-counting assignment group. Students begin with 3 Core revision tokens.

5.4 Core revision tokens

Students begin with 3 Core revision tokens. Tokens are for Core work only. They are not used for Advanced work.

  • A token reopens needed Core item(s) for a module group after the module items lock.

  • If a student missed most or all of a Core module, adjust dates for past and current module so the student has a realistic path through the missed module and the next module.

  • If the student only needs one revision, reopen the relevant item rather than redesigning the whole week.

  • Token due dates may be flexible. Use student-specific dates to communicate the recovery plan.

  • Early in the course, if a student clearly misunderstood that Not Ready requires revision, prioritize recovery and communication over rigid enforcement.

  • When applying a token, update the token count, change the relevant student-specific due/availability dates, message the student, and log a private Gradebook note.

5.5 Core grading expectations

Core file-submission assignments receive the most detailed feedback. The instructor should scan code enough to catch obvious problems, but the main grading focus is the student’s memos, documentation, file correctness, document formatting, evidence of learning, and submission of the correct artifact. Practice Space quizzes support code-checking because students can retry them to mastery.

Common Not Ready reason Instructor response
Wrong file or missing file Mark Not Ready. Explain which artifact is required and request resubmission.
Incomplete file or old/failed HTML knit Mark Not Ready if the submitted artifact does not show the completed work. Explain how to verify the output before submitting.
Missing/thin memos Mark Not Ready when memos do not show the learning process. Ask for concrete comments/reflections tied to the code.
Poor document formatting Use judgment. Mark Not Ready if unreadable or missing required structure; otherwise provide improvement feedback for next time.
AI-generated or copied-looking work without evidence of student process Treat as a learning/revision issue first. Mark Not Ready, explain the concern, and require work that shows the student’s own process.

Use the SpeedGrader to enter traditional and rubric grade, write feedback. When the student earns “Not Ready”, click “Reassign Assignment” after leaving feedback.

5.6 Core feedback voice

Core feedback should be student-facing, specific, warm, and direct. On Complete work, name what the student did well and one or two ways to keep improving. On Not Ready work, explain what needs revision and communicate that high expectations are paired with confidence the student can meet them. The Boise State AI feedback chatbot may be used as a drafting aid, but the instructor remains the grader and must edit the feedback before posting.

5.7 Advanced grading expectations

Advanced work should be graded promptly but lightly. Most Advanced submissions should be marked Complete when there is good-faith completion. Do not give heavy feedback on every Advanced item. Save detailed coaching for Core work unless the Advanced submission needs correction.

  • Use Not Ready for wrong uploads, major misunderstanding, very low-effort work, unclear evidence of completion, or work that does not appear to show the student’s own learning process.

  • If the problem is a small submission issue caught early, message the student and make a reasonable correction window.

  • Advanced work has no token pathway. Once locked, missing Advanced work stays locked.

  • For discussions, monitor enough to protect the learning environment and clarify when needed, but let students talk to each other.

5.8 Student AI use

AI-use expectations are embedded in the student-facing pages, activities, rubrics, and course chatbots. The instructor’s role is to coach students toward better AI habits, especially early in the course when students may arrive with unproductive copy/paste habits.

If a submission appears copied from AI and does not show the student’s own learning process, mark it Not Ready, explain the concern directly, and require revision. Do not over-invest in proving intent. Focus on whether the submission meets the learning, documentation, and artifact specifications.

5.9 Final course grade audit

During the final week, audit each student’s final bundle status before submitting official grades. Use a visible informational Final Grade assignment in Canvas so students can see the instructor-determined final grade before official submission. This assignment page is not published until final grading takes place.

6 Communication and Student Support

Student outreach should happen primarily through Canvas. There is no general Course Questions discussion forum in this course. Use Canvas Inbox/Gradebook messages, assignment comments, Gradebook Notes, and booked Zoom meetings as the main support channels.

6.1 Canvas communication workflow

6.2 Week 1 participation and early intervention

Week 1 is an early-success checkpoint. Participation means meaningful Canvas engagement and submitted work, not just enrollment.

Timing Action
Wednesday of Week 1 Contact students who have not logged in or submitted early work.
Friday of Week 1 Send a second check-in if needed and continue normal Friday outreach for missing Core work.
Monday of Week 2 Submit academic alerts for students who have not participated meaningfully and have not responded to instructor outreach.
After Week 1 Watch for students who stopped participating. Message them on Fridays and follow up on Mondays. Submit academic alerts for non-responding students on Mondays.

Late enrollment. Do not approve late-enrollment exceptions once the 7-week course has started. In this course, late entry usually sets students up to fail because the course moves too quickly and builds on early setup work.

6.3 Ongoing student check-ins

Required outreach is focused on students who are inactive, missing Core work, falling behind, or not responding. A mid-course bundle-status check is recommended around the end of Week 3 or start of Week 4, especially in a term when students appear confused about Core/Advanced progress.

Level What the instructor does When to use it
Minimum Contact only at-risk, inactive, or nonresponsive students. Always.
Targeted Contact students who are active but not excelling or seem frequently stuck. Recommended.
Full Send every student a personalized status note: likely bundle path, what is going well, what to do next, and ask a reflection prompt. Excellent practice when time allows.

6.4 Meetings with students

The current course design offers students a Book a Meeting page linked from multiple places in Canvas. The current workflow uses Google Calendar appointment scheduling for 30-minute Zoom meetings. A future instructor may change the workflow, but must preserve a clear meeting-booking pathway or update all related links and buttons.

7 Discussion Groups

Discussion groups support belonging, timing alignment, and manageable peer interaction. They should be large enough to create conversation but small enough to avoid overwhelming students in a combined Canvas site.

7.1 Group setup rules

  • Use stable Canvas discussion groups for peer-review and many reflection activities.

  • Create groups near the start of Week 1 after observing early participation patterns.

  • Set groups size to between 15-20 students.

  • Week one set up group membership by student activity. Early submitters with early submitters, slower-starting students with slower-starting students. The purpose is timing alignment, not labeling students.

  • Make sure one student is the only poster for their group and left in a empty room.

7.2 Whole-class discussions

Discussion type Recommended setup Reason
Hack-A-Prompt Whole-class discussion The activity is optional Advanced work and already splits by pathway. Smaller groups can leave students alone in a discussion space.
Git/GitHub help-style discussion Whole-class discussion Participation is limited to students choosing that Advanced activity and the discussion functions partly as a support forum.
Peer-review and reflections Stable discussion groups Students benefit from recurring peers and manageable feedback spaces.

8.2 Weekly learning-skill guide

Week / module Primary course focus Instructor emphasis
Week 1 / Module 1 Tenacity with Resilient Beginnings Advanced work Help students start strong with RStudio, basic R commands, scripts, comments, saved files, and early workflow habits. Reinforce that beginner errors are expected. Praise students who notice what happened, test a fix, document what changed, and keep going. Watch closely for non-participation, setup problems, and early AI copy/paste habits.
Week 2 / Module 2 Transparency with Innovation Nursery Advanced work Emphasize clear, reviewable R work: vectors, object structures, R Notebook habits, visible checks, resource citation, and written explanations. Praise students who leave a trail of what they ran, what output appeared, what they checked, and what they concluded. Watch for coercion confusion, indexing/name confusion, case-sensitivity errors, and thin documentation.
Week 3 / Module 3 Courage with Hack-A-Prompt Round 1 Advanced work Reinforce troubleshooting, help-seeking, missing-value decisions, and the courage to stop when output or rendering does not make sense. Praise students who read errors carefully, inspect missing values, document choices, and ask clear questions. Watch for failed renders, hidden NA problems, weak memos, and students moving ahead without understanding the issue.
Week 4 / Module 4 Autonomy with GitHub Advanced work opening Help students take more ownership of file management, importing data, selecting rows/columns/values, and verifying that objects are what they expect. Reinforce that autonomy means managing the workflow responsibly, not working in isolation. Watch for working-directory errors, wrong-file imports, indexing mistakes, and Git/GitHub setup issues for students choosing that Advanced path.
Week 5 / Advanced bridge week GitHub workflow and Hack-A-Prompt Round 2 preparation/launch Support students who are continuing Git/GitHub setup and students who are choosing the tidyverse Hack-A-Prompt pathway. Emphasize verification checkpoints, careful reading of instructions, and realistic pacing on Advanced options. Watch for students who want a B/A bundle but are not yet completing enough Advanced work, and remind them that Advanced activities are choices, not a requirement to do everything.
Week 6 / Module 6 Curiosity with Hack-A-Prompt Round 2 Advanced work Encourage students to ask clear data questions, recode deliberately, compare groups, and use summaries to investigate patterns. Praise useful questions, careful observations, and thoughtful interpretation of grouped results. Watch for students who create variables without checking them, summarize before verifying groups, or assume tidyverse pipelines are correct just because they run.
Week 7 / Module 7 Thoroughness with Wildfire Advanced work Support finishing well: probability simulation, repeated outcomes, ggplot2 visualizations, rendered Quarto reports, peer feedback, revision, and final grade auditing. Praise students who check whether results make sense, choose appropriate visualizations, explain output clearly, and avoid superficial last-minute work. Watch for failed renders, incomplete files, weak interpretation, missing final reflections, and unresolved Not Ready/Core completion issues.

10 Course Maintenance and Change Memos

This course is designed to improve over time. Instructors are encouraged to make small clarifying edits when student work reveals a repeated point of confusion. Larger or structural edits should be documented so the copied-forward course remains coherent.

10.1 Running change memo

Maintain a running course-change memo. Each entry only needs to answer the following questions:

  • Where was the change made?

  • When was it changed?

  • Why was it changed?

  • What changed?

  • Does this affect future offerings?

Change memo/examples link: Change memo examples

10.2 What to document

Change type Document?
Tiny typo or punctuation correction Usually no.
Clarifying a confusing sentence, instruction, rubric language, or due-date wording If it affects student interpretation.
Changing dates, locks, prerequisites, or module requirements Yes. Also verify access in Student View.
Adding/removing assignments, quizzes, discussions, rubrics, or outcomes Yes. This is structural.
Changing grading logic, token policy, bundle requirements, or Advanced counts Yes. This affects the course model and must be reflected in student-facing materials.
Changing meeting-booking, communication, or announcement workflows Yes if linked from course pages. Run link validation.
